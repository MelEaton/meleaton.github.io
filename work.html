Pulling Together

Kaiapoi

March 2024

Cure Boat Club is the oldest rowing club in New Zealand, and commissioned this 200m² mural for their Kaiapoi clubrooms as part of an ongoing rebuild following the 2011 earthquakes. Renovations are tracking toward a $2.2 million restoration goal, and the brief asked for a design that celebrated the Kaiapoi River, with the club's signature purple as the dominant colour.

OTW built the mural to do double duty: a striking exterior wall, and a piece of brand collateral the club could put straight to work. It's now the backdrop they reach for whenever they need a photo that reads instantly as "Cure Boat Club," whether that's a sponsor visit or a fundraising appeal. The result has lifted the club's profile in a prominent riverside spot.

It's a fair snapshot of how OTW approaches brand work: grounded in colour and story, built at a scale that holds up as a long-term asset rather than a one-off decoration.

Pixel Party

Rangiora

December 2021

Ten Forward was a brand-new after-school and birthday-party venue in Rangiora, built around console gaming, tabletop games and clubs like D&D and Minecraft. They needed the space to feel unmistakably on-brand from the moment customers walked in, on a modest budget and across roughly 20m² of wall space.

Rather than spread the budget thin, OTW concentrated it into one bold feature wall, then carried smaller character details through the rest of the room to create an all-over effect without the cost of an all-over mural. The brief was as much about identity as decoration: kids needed to recognise instantly that this was a gaming space made for them.

It worked. Customers reported kids hunting the walls for their favourite characters, and there was no question left about what the business was or who it was for. Concentrate spend where it has the most impact, then let smaller touches do the rest: a useful approach to any tight commercial budget.

Shear Havoc

Burnside

August 2022

Shear Havoc, a hair salon built specifically for children, was expanding into a neighbouring space and wanted the new room to feel as engaging and on-brand as the rest of the salon. Cartoon characters were core to the brief, since familiar faces help young children sit still and feel comfortable through a haircut, but the salon didn't want a wall of competing colours fighting their branding for attention.

OTW worked through a wide cast of characters within a limited palette pulled from the salon's own brand colours, so the room read as cohesive rather than chaotic. Layout had to track the fit-out as it developed too, with chairs, mirrors and shelving going in around the design, which meant staying closely coordinated with the salon's own renovation schedule rather than working to a fixed, empty room.

The result tied the new space firmly into the existing brand and calmed what could easily have been a visually busy room. Staff were thrilled with how well it matched the rest of the salon, and customers' kids love the characters: proof that a strong brief and a disciplined colour palette don't have to pull against each other.

You Rock

Rangiora

February 2022

Riddell Gym is a home-based personal training studio that trainer Katie built out of a converted garage, working mostly with women. She wanted one bold design that would make the space feel like a destination rather than a converted garage, and she wanted a slogan or phrase worked into it.

With equipment taking up much of the floor and wall space, there wasn't room for anything elaborate. The brief came down to delivering maximum impact through a single confident design that still carried Katie's own words, and OTW treated the limited space as the design problem to solve rather than something to work around.

Katie was thrilled with the result, describing the shift as going from a basic converted garage to an inspiring, dynamic workout space. When there's only one wall and one shot at it, the brief and the budget both come down to the same thing: one strong idea, executed cleanly.

In Our Hands

Rangiora

May 2021

This mural was Mel's submission to the Keep New Zealand Beautiful and Resene Nature Mural competition, made on behalf of Nest Arts Collective, a group of North Canterbury artists set up to nurture and promote creativity in the region. Mel identified a tag-targeted carpark in need of rejuvenation and rallied the collective around it, securing additional funding through Creative Communities to get the 150m² project off the ground. The design draws on Nest's own branding and birds, layered with a conservation message: a Haast eagle representing what's come before, the hands of Ranginui and Papatūānuku cradling a nest of eggs to represent what we should care for, and native birds, reptiles and insects representing diversity.

Running a mural this size in an active carpark meant negotiating directly with council for traffic management and health and safety clearance, then building a schedule around minimising disruption to parking and public access. A temporary on-site storage shed kept tools and materials secure without leaving anything exposed overnight.

The carpark has been transformed from a regularly tagged eyesore into a backdrop that local cafes and businesses now use in their own photography. It was even added to Pokémon Go as a local landmark. As OTW's first large exterior project, it set the template for managing scale, council process and public access that every job since has built on.

Flowing With Whio

Rangiora

October 2023

This mural was entered into the Keep New Zealand Beautiful and Resene Nature Mural competition, the same award In Our Hands was made for. It began with Mel approaching council directly to ask if there was a wall that could use some attention. They flagged a toilet block at Dudley Park, a busy reserve next to a skate park, hockey ground and cricket field, and a regular target for tagging. The finished 35m² design wraps around all four sides of the block, with different vantage points of whio (blue duck) on the water, viewed from the front, above and below.

Paint quality mattered as much as the design here, since the brief was really about deterring graffiti as much as decorating a wall. Mid-project, the block was tagged before the mural was even finished, forcing a restoration of completed work before continuing. OTW finished the piece with an anti-graffiti coating so council could clean any future tags without damaging the artwork underneath.

Since completion, reports indicate a real drop in tagging on the block. It's a pattern OTW has seen elsewhere too: blank walls invite tags, detailed murals don't, and on the rare occasion one does appear, it's far less visible against a full design than against bare paint.

Urban Oasis

Rangiora

November 2024

Chorus runs an ongoing Cabinet Art programme, turning street-level telecommunications cabinets into small public artworks across urban and industrial areas. For this 3m² cabinet, OTW set out to create a trompe l'oeil effect: painting the metal box to look as though it had been filled with water, plants and a tropical fish, so it reads as a tiny aquarium rather than a cabinet at all.

The brief, as with every Chorus cabinet, was simple: bring art to a public space without needing anything special from council or Chorus to install it. The technical work sat in the surface prep (cleaning and priming the metal properly so paint holds) and in the optical illusion itself, where getting the water and glass effect convincing enough to fool a passer-by took careful work with light, reflection and depth.

It's a modest job, but a useful one: proof OTW can take a flat, unglamorous surface and turn it into something people stop and look twice at.

Hidden Treasure

Fernside

January 2024

This Chorus Cabinet Art commission started with the site itself. The cabinet sat tucked into the corner of a large hedge, mostly out of view. Rather than fight that placement, OTW designed around it, painting a dragon that looks like it's hiding in the hedge, using the cabinet's awkward position as the punchline of the concept instead of a problem to disguise.

As with other Chorus cabinets, the technical groundwork was straightforward but essential: proper cleaning and priming to get paint holding on metal for the long term. The creative work was in reading the site before designing anything, letting the surroundings shape the concept rather than applying a design that ignored its context.

The lesson here scales well beyond a small cabinet: a site visit isn't just about measuring a wall, it's about finding what's already interesting about a location and building the concept around it.

Tui Song

Rangiora

October 2022

This Chorus cabinet sat next to a glass balustrade already decorated with vinyl decals of flax and cabbage trees. Instead of designing in isolation, OTW picked up those existing motifs and carried them onto the cabinet itself, adding a tui to complete the scene, so the finished piece reads as one continuous moment rather than two unrelated installations sitting side by side.

The job itself was modest, a 1.5m² cabinet with the usual metal prep of cleaning and priming before painting, but the thinking behind it is the more useful takeaway. Plenty of street-level sites already have design elements in place: signage, decals, planting, fencing. Treating those as part of the brief rather than working around them produces a far more cohesive result than treating every surface as a blank canvas.

OTW applies this same habit at every scale: look at what's already on site, and use it, rather than competing with it.

Woven History

Kaiapoi

October 2023

Kaiapoi's history as home to the wool mills that produced the country's well-known wool blankets gave OTW the concept for this Chorus cabinet: a patchwork design drawing directly on research already done for Mammoth Wolley, another Kaiapoi commission inspired by the same wool mill history.

Not every reaction was positive. The cabinet sits in a residential subdivision, and several residents approached Mel directly, unhappy about the work in progress, even as more people stopped to compliment it. Rather than ignore the criticism or take it personally, OTW engaged Chorus and the Waimakariri District Council directly, using diplomacy to help the more disgruntled critics understand the project's intent, while accepting that not every piece of public art will land for every viewer.

It's a useful case study in something every public art commission eventually runs into: managing differing public opinion in real time, on site, while keeping the client and council on side.

Ice

Christchurch

November 2020

Ice was created for Wild In Art's Pop-up Penguins trail, a large-scale public art and fundraising event where decorated penguin sculptures are displayed across the city before being auctioned for charity. The design grew out of Mel's personal interest in painting large-scale gemstones, with the title playing on "ice" as a term for diamonds and as the natural habitat of a penguin.

Painting precise diamond facets onto a curved, three-dimensional sculpture took a lot of careful masking-tape work to keep every geometric edge clean across a surface that never sits flat. It's a different discipline to painting a wall: every angle changes how the facets read, and the geometry has to hold up convincingly from every direction the public will view it from.

The wider event raised over $1 million at auction. Ice is proof OTW can take a genuinely difficult three-dimensional surface and a fairly abstract creative concept, and execute both to a standard that holds up in a high-profile charity auction.

Birds of a Feather

Kaiapoi

November 2020

This design was created for Wild In Art's Pop-up Penguins trail, the same Christchurch fundraising event behind another OTW piece, Ice, where decorated penguin sculptures are displayed around the city before being auctioned for charity. It was developed as part of Nest Arts Collective, drawing its inspiration straight from Nest's own branding and bird imagery. Unlike a solo commission, this one involved coordinating a small group of painters to bring the design to life on the sculpture.

Mel pencilled up the drawing and oversaw the painting process, working alongside the group rather than directing from the sidelines, to keep a single consistent style across the finished piece despite multiple hands being involved. That's a meaningfully different skill to painting solo: briefing other painters clearly enough that the result reads as one artist's work, and staying hands-on enough to catch anything that drifts from the design.

The event raised over $1 million at auction across all the sculptures involved. This is the clearest evidence OTW can lead a small team on a larger project and still protect design consistency, useful for any brief that needs more than one painter to deliver on schedule.

Mammoth Wolley

Kaiapoi

November 2023

Painted for Wild In Art's Elmer Christchurch trail, Mammoth Wolley started with a chain of wordplay: from Elmer the patchwork elephant, to a patchwork blanket, to a wool blanket, to a woolly mammoth. Turning that idea into a finished design meant a genuine research process: digging into the history of Kiwi wool blankets and the mills that made them, including the ones based in Kaiapoi, and gathering original wool mill label designs from across the country to recreate as a patchwork history across the sculpture's surface.

Painting a three-dimensional sculpture brings its own technical demands, the same ones OTW worked through on other Wild In Art pieces, but the standout part of this project is the depth of research behind the concept, the kind of groundwork a story-driven commission deserves before a single brushstroke goes on.

The result resonated enough that Zero Height Safety, the event's main sponsor, bought Mammoth Wolley at auction. A well-researched concept doesn't just look good: it can end up the most commercially desirable piece in a room full of competing artworks.

Louie Braille

Christchurch

May 2024

Commissioned by the New Zealand Blind Foundation, this project asked OTW to give a decommissioned "Trudy" coin collection box (a dog sculpture used for street fundraising) a fresh design ahead of auction. The brief called for something that would raise awareness of blindness as well as funds, so the design pays tribute to Louis Braille, inventor of the reading and writing system used by blind people worldwide. Two messages, "A world of imagination is at your fingertips" and "knowledge is power," are worked into the surface using raised dots, alongside texture elements and fingerprint motifs.

Beyond the usual material research needed to confirm the paint would bond properly to the sculpture's plastic surface, the real work was getting the braille itself right. That meant researching it carefully enough to encode genuine, accurate messages onto a physical surface, rather than a decorative approximation of the alphabet.

The auction this piece was part of raised over $43,000. It shows OTW taking a values-led brief seriously enough to do real research before designing, rather than treating an awareness message as a slogan slapped over decoration.

Flock Together

Loburn

May 2026

Loburn Primary School commissioned this 24m² mural to give the whole school a central, shared motif and capture its identity. A student-led committee brought a genuinely large volume of ideas to the table, and the brief became as much about facilitation as design: merging dozens of individual ideas into a smaller set of unified symbols, so every contribution was represented without the final design becoming visually overloaded.

Getting students physically involved in the painting mattered too, but without requiring any particular skill or a complexity the timeline couldn't absorb. OTW solved that with handprints: each class, named after a bird, added a handprint to their own class bird, giving every student a genuine, visible stake in the finished mural without slowing the project down.

The school's own feedback sums up the result: "Attention to detail, great consultation and superb artwork." Not many projects manage a consultation process this open and still land a coherent final design, but that's what happened here.

Around The Tree

Rangiora

July 2025

Rangiora Borough School needed a mural that could capture the dual identity of their Ngā Rākau e Rua bilingual setting, as part of a wider project that also included a new native garden and a pou sculpture. The brief required the design to bring together the marae, the school tree, the school's heart emblem, and a range of native birds, flora and fauna specific to the region: a lot of meaning to fit into one 60m², double-level piece.

The school wanted to unveil the finished mural at Matariki, in the middle of winter, and the wall itself needed a lift to reach. To manage both constraints at once, OTW painted the bulk of the mural off-site on ply panels in a workshop, cutting the amount of time needed on-site for installation and removing weather and access as risks to the deadline.

The mural has become a major feature of the school, described as a taonga of their identity. Planning production around a fixed deadline and a difficult site, rather than letting either force a compromise, is what made it work.

Our Stars

Rangiora

November 2022

Entered into the Keep New Zealand Beautiful and Resene Wall Worthy competition, this project asked Tūtira Ashgrove School what mattered to their identity and narrative, then built a process to answer that properly. OTW ran a school-wide mural competition to gather design ideas, then developed those into a final concept that included a "gallery" element: sectioned panels where small groups of students painted their own pieces on subjects that mattered to them, from sport and theatre to gardening, reading and music.

That structure meant the final 15m² mural wasn't just designed for the school, it was partly designed and made by the school, while still reading as one coherent, professionally finished piece rather than a patchwork of unrelated student work.

The mural is now used regularly to share the school's identity, both internally and with visitors, and has become a shared touchstone for the whole community. It shows a particular skill: building real participation into a project without losing creative and quality control over the result.

Tiaki Tuna

Halswell

May 2026

Halswell Primary School had recently completed classroom work on how drain water affects rivers and the tuna (eels) living in them, and wanted a mural that reinforced that learning rather than just decorated a wall. OTW built a two-part response: a 15m² playground groundwork piece showing tuna swimming through a painted river, and a connected wall piece on panels.

Groundwork has to survive constant foot traffic, so the design used a hard-wearing "walk-on" paint with added grit to reduce slip risk, a different technical requirement entirely to painting a vertical wall. To keep the educational link genuine rather than decorative, OTW created a worksheet so students could submit their own tuna designs, which were then recreated directly into the ground painting. That gave the final artwork actual student authorship rather than a generic eel motif chosen on their behalf.

The result reinforced what the school set out to teach: that what goes into a drain ends up in a river, and that the tuna living there are worth protecting. This is what a genuine, curriculum-linked co-design process looks like when it's paired with the right technical material for the surface.

Natures Playground

Rangiora

June 2022

This project was entered into the Keep New Zealand Beautiful and Resene Wall Worthy competition, the same award behind Our Stars and Weaving Together. It set out to make Rangiora Play Centre's playground fence more visually engaging for the kids who use it. At 7.2m², it was a straightforward brief, but the centre had no extra budget for the plywood needed to mount the artwork on their fencing.

Rather than treat that as a dead end, the centre secured the plywood as a donation from a local lumberyard, and OTW built the project around that in-kind support. It's a small but useful reminder that community projects often aren't blocked by a lack of money so much as a lack of someone asking the right local business for the right material at the right time.

The finished artwork ties neatly into the playground space and has held up well. It's a useful pattern for any tight-budget brief: OTW doesn't just paint to the funding that's already secured, but helps identify and ask for the in-kind support that can close the gap.

Weaving Together

Rangiora

October 2021

Little Pepper Tree Preschool wanted their carpark entrance to feel welcoming, and entered the Keep New Zealand Beautiful and Resene Wall Worthy competition to help fund it. The 15m² design draws on flax and weaving, built around the whakataukī "Whiria te tāngata: weave the people together," and was constructed as panels of exterior treated plywood fixed directly to the existing fence posts.

The bigger achievement here was involving every preschooler in the painting itself, despite their age. OTW had the children create a handprint background texture first, then layered the harakeke weaving design on top of it. The technique needed no artistic skill from the kids at all, just paint and a hand, while still giving every child a genuine, visible part in the finished mural.

This is what designing the process looks like, not just the image: finding a way to include very young children meaningfully, without compromising the quality or coherence of the final piece.

Playtime Bugs

Cust

October 2022

Cust Primary School wanted playground games with a consistent, fun theme and a genuine educational purpose, rather than a generic hopscotch grid. OTW built a full set of bug-themed games: a hopscotch dragonfly, an alphabet millipede, copycat ladybirds and an obstacle course of butterflies, worms and grasshoppers, tying every game back to one coherent theme across the playground.

Groundwork is a genuinely difficult surface to paint well: it's walked on constantly and weathers fast. OTW used bold, simple shapes that hold up better to wear than fine detail would, and handed the school a catalogue of every colour used, so they can touch up high-wear areas themselves between repaints rather than needing to call OTW back for every scuff.

The playground has held up well under high foot traffic and is, by the school's own account, a lot of fun. The colour catalogue is worth noting too: a small but meaningful bit of aftercare, handing the school the tools to maintain the work themselves rather than just finishing the job and walking away.

Growing Together

Kaiapoi

February 2026

The Chris Ruth Centre, a Kaiapoi facility supporting disabled residents, had a shipping container sitting in their carpark, right beside their gardens, that had become a visual eyesore. The brief was to beautify it and tie it into the surrounding garden environment, which OTW did with native birds and flora alongside the text "Growing Together," a message that connects directly to the centre's mission of helping residents build confidence and mana.

Shipping containers are an awkward surface: metal, shaped, and not built with art in mind, so a bold design mattered more than fine detail to make the concept read clearly. The project also gave OTW the chance to work with and mentor youth volunteers through the full mural process, from concept to brush.

That mentoring relationship is arguably the most valuable outcome of the project: a genuine investment in training the next generation of muralists, something OTW hopes to keep building into future community work.

Turangawaewae

Kaiapoi

February 2026

This 35m² mural, also for the Chris Ruth Centre (a Kaiapoi facility supporting disabled residents), set out to beautify the space and give residents a stronger sense of place and identity. The first concept direction focused on empowerment and the people themselves, but that approach inadvertently drew attention to the very disabilities the centre's team didn't want foregrounded. Rather than push ahead with a design the client wasn't comfortable with, OTW pivoted to a different idea entirely: a landscape inspired by the centre's own surroundings, reframing the mural around belonging and place instead of the residents directly.

That willingness to change direction mid-concept, in response to genuine client feedback rather than ego, is the real takeaway here. As with the centre's other mural, Growing Together, the project also involved mentoring youth volunteers through the painting process, continuing OTW's effort to bring new people into mural work.

This is how OTW handles a design that isn't landing: not by defending it, but by listening and finding a better answer.
